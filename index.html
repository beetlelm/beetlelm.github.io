Beetle: A Bilingual Model Suite for Modelling Second-Language Processing

Suchir Salhan1, Catherine Arnett2, James A. Michaelov3, Paula Buttery1
1University of Cambridge, 2EleutherAI, 3University of Oxford
EMNLP 2026
Paper arXiv Code Models & Data
TL;DR: We introduce Beetle, a controlled language-model pretraining framework in which tokeniser, target language, training budget and exposure structure are each independently manipulable. Using Beetle we train and release 285 bilingual and 45 monolingual open-source models with rich checkpoints across a range of exposure schedules, data scales and first languages. Evaluating against human bilingual and second-language reading-time and grammaticality-judgement data, we find that staged, temporally structured curricula consistently improve alignment with language-learner reading time over balanced bilingual training — with the largest gains at smaller data scales and for typologically closer language pairs.

Bilingual language models offer a controlled setting for studying how training conditions shape second-language (L2) behaviour. But prior work typically varies exposure structure, scale, and architecture at once, making it difficult to attribute any effect to a single factor. If a staged bilingual schedule helps, is it the schedule, the amount of second-language data, or the model — and under which first language?

Beetle is designed to make these questions answerable. It is a controlled pretraining framework in which the tokeniser, target language, training budget, and exposure structure are each independently manipulable. Holding the others fixed makes the effect of first language, curriculum and data scale attributable to one cause — enabling systematic, comparable experimentation. The exposure structure is varied through five curricula, B1–B5, that change when the second language is introduced and how the per-language data distribution unfolds across training steps.

Using this framework we train and release 330 open models — 285 bilingual and 45 monolingual — with rich checkpoints across five curricula, three data scales (100M / 2B / 24B tokens) and 21 first languages. We then evaluate the models against human data on bilingual and second-language reading-time prediction, grammaticality judgement, and cross-lingual structural priming.

Across these evaluations, staged and temporally structured curricula (B2, B3, B5) consistently align better with language-learner reading time than balanced bilingual training (B1). The advantage of structured exposure is largest at smaller data scales — where how the input is ordered matters most — and for typologically closer language pairs. A dissociation emerges between online reading time and offline grammaticality judgement: the differences that are sharp for reading-time alignment are smaller for minimal-pair discrimination.

Cross-lingual structural priming — a signature of abstract syntactic representation in humans — is robustly present in the models and persists across data scale. Together, these findings position the Beetle models as tools to help move computational psycholinguistics beyond its prevailing monolingual, English-centric focus, toward models of human bilingual processing and cross-lingual learning dynamics, while supporting community-based development of controlled model families. Every released checkpoint loads directly with HuggingFace Transformers via from_pretrained.
